## 4. Competency questions (STAR format)

Use the headings as the "Situation / Task / Action / Result" scaffold. Each is 150–250 words; trim to the limit.

### Teamwork — "Describe a time you worked effectively in a team."
**Situation:** At NoorDX I worked in a multidisciplinary team of biologists, clinicians and data specialists, each with different vocabularies and priorities.
**Task:** We needed genomic datasets cleaned and validated to a standard the analysts could trust, on a schedule set by the clinical side.
**Action:** I agreed the quality checks with the analysts up front so we shared a definition of "clean", built the pipeline in Bash so it was reproducible rather than one-off, and documented each stage so a colleague could rerun or audit it without me. When a dataset failed a check I raised it with the relevant specialist rather than guessing at a fix.
**Result:** The workflows were reused across later datasets and handed over cleanly when I left. What I took from it is that teams across disciplines work when the interfaces, in this case the quality criteria, are agreed explicitly.

### Problem solving — "Describe a complex technical problem you solved."
**Situation:** My final-year project modelled a double-sided LCL-compensated capacitive power transfer system in MATLAB.
**Task:** Early simulations gave unstable or physically implausible results, and I had to establish whether the fault was the model, the numerical method or my parameters.
**Action:** I broke the system into parts and tested each against known analytical behaviour, implemented the dynamics with an RK4 solver and compared it against MATLAB's ODE functions, and swept the compensation parameters methodically rather than adjusting several at once. I kept a log of every run so I could see which change produced which effect.
**Result:** I isolated the issue to [step size / a parameter / an equation error], produced a stable, validated model, and was able to analyse the design trade-offs the project was actually about. The habit of changing one thing at a time and recording it is now how I approach every debugging task.

### Learning quickly / adaptability
**Situation:** I joined NoorDX with an engineering background and no formal bioinformatics training.
**Task:** I needed to become productive with command-line genomic tooling and the domain's quality standards quickly.
**Action:** I worked through structured EMBL-EBI training, around fifty modules in total, alongside the job, and applied each new technique directly to the datasets I was handling. I asked the specialists to check my understanding early rather than after I had built something on a misunderstanding.
**Result:** Within weeks I was building and documenting pipelines others relied on. It showed me I can move into an unfamiliar technical area fast if I pair structured learning with immediate practice.

### Attention to detail / working in a regulated environment
**Situation:** At Strensham Road Surgery I handle patient records, recruitment paperwork and compliance tasks in an NHS setting.
**Task:** Errors there have real consequences: a mis-filed record, a missed compliance deadline or a confidentiality breach.
**Action:** I built structured tracking for operational and compliance tasks so nothing relied on memory, double-checked records against source documents, and escalated anything ambiguous rather than assuming.
**Result:** The practice has a clearer view of what is due and what is done, and I have developed a habit of careful, checkable work that transfers directly to engineering documentation, test records and safety-critical systems.

### Communication / explaining technical work to non-specialists
**Situation:** Across the NHS and NoorDX roles I regularly explained technical or process issues to people without that background: patients, clinicians, biologists.
**Task:** Get the essential point across without jargon, so they could make a decision.
**Action:** I lead with what the other person needs to decide or do, then give the minimum technical reasoning behind it, and confirm their understanding by asking what they will do next rather than whether they understood.
**Result:** Fewer repeat questions and faster decisions. In engineering I apply the same approach to documentation: written for the reader who has to act on it.

### Working under pressure / prioritising
**Situation:** At the surgery I handle time-sensitive patient requests while also running recruitment and compliance processes with fixed deadlines.
**Task:** Keep urgent patient needs first without letting the deadline-driven work slip.
**Action:** I triage on arrival, deal with anything safety-related or time-critical immediately, and keep the longer tasks in a tracked list with dates so they progress in gaps rather than being forgotten.
**Result:** Both streams get done reliably. I prefer visible tracking over heroics, which is also how I would approach a commissioning schedule or test campaign.

### Initiative / going beyond the role
**Situation:** The surgery relied on informal methods to follow operational and compliance tasks.
**Task:** No one had asked me to change it, but the lack of visibility was causing avoidable chasing.
**Action:** I set up structured tracking in Microsoft Office covering task, owner and due date and brought it to the team as a proposal rather than imposing it.
**Result:** It was adopted and improved visibility of what was outstanding. Small, concrete tooling changes are often the highest-value thing a junior person can offer.

### Hands-on technical / embedded example
**Situation:** HomeAir, an ESP32-based IoT prototype combining sensing with system logic.
**Task:** Get sensing, logic and connectivity working together reliably on real hardware.
**Action:** I developed it incrementally, getting each sensor and subsystem working and verified before integrating, and used systematic debugging when readings or behaviour did not match expectations, checking wiring, timing and code in turn.
**Result:** A working prototype and a much clearer understanding of the gap between a design that is correct on paper and one that works on a bench.
